# Tlahtōlli: Evidence, attribution, and Nahua language stewardship

Status: working research standard, October 28, 2026. **No contributor, linguistic expert, Nahuatl-speaking community, or Indigenous organization has endorsed this project or reviewed its transcriptions unless separately identified with permission.**

## Scope and attribution

The *Florentine Codex* was produced through the knowledge and work of Nahua authors, elders, and tlahcuilohqueh, together with Bernardino de Sahagún in sixteenth-century colonial Mexico. Credit the Nahua contributors and the manuscript's makers, not just Sahagún. [Getty manuscript citation and personnel](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/en/).

The project's first target is the historical written Nahuatl of the Codex. It must not be presented as the sole authentic variety of Nahuatl or as a replacement for contemporary varieties. Modern speakers' expertise is essential, but a modern dialect's syntax, sounds, and vocabulary cannot be silently applied to a particular sixteenth-century manuscript.

## Evidence claims

- **Observed image fragment:** A human or visual-model check of a bounded folio region, including uncertainty. This is not a completed diplomatic transcription.
- **Manuscript-transcribed line:** Every handwritten glyph and line break independently checked against a source-image at adequate resolution, with image locator, line number or bounding coordinates, and unresolved characters retained. Still does **not** certify morphology.
- **Grammatically reviewed:** An explicit analysis using historical grammar and dictionary evidence, alternatives, and a named competent reviewer. No anonymous AI-only expert sign-off.
- **Meaning or translation reviewed:** A separate review of passage-level context by a qualified reader, not solely dictionary lookups or reliance on the Spanish parallel column.
- **Approved bilingual training pair:** Only after transcription, morphology, translation, reuse-rights, and evaluation-group checks all pass. The browser's self-declared review status is never sufficient.

Current reality: **two folios inspected visually; five bounded image observations; zero fully certified manuscript lines; zero approved Codex bilingual training pairs; no neural model trained.**

## Ethical handling

1. Give precise source and researcher attribution. Recheck licenses independently for manuscript photographs, modern digital editions, spoken recordings and translations.
2. For any contemporary Nahuatl speech or community-produced material, seek informed, specific permission and establish the contributor's control over public release, withdrawal, attribution and use in model training. Public online access is not the same as consent.
3. Create a community advisory/review process with genuine participation and appropriate compensation before claiming to produce an authoritative educational translator. No token acknowledgment or invented endorsement.
4. Preserve dialect/era labels and allow alternative readings. Explain where uncertain grammar cannot be resolved.
5. Keep corrections traceable and avoid erasing old disputed readings. Record when AI, OCR, or editorial inference participated.
6. Publish error rates from held-out manuscripts, not only examples the tool has already seen. Do not describe word frequency or headword matching as translation accuracy.
7. Never suggest that Tlahtōlli has authority to prescribe Nahuatl usage to its speakers. Its role is evidence-based access to historical sources.
